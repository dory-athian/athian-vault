Grounding Context

Topic Context

Product: Athian Compliance — an AI-native, DSL-first compliance platform for CPG companies managing Scope 3 supplier emissions. Compliance requirements are machine-readable: typed with unit, threshold, time period, required data, and accepted sources. AI handles pre-assessment; the compliance specialist is reviewer and approver.

Structural problems driving this ideation: One evaluator at volume against many suppliers. Evidence arrives in heterogeneous formats. Requirements expire, renew, and escalate. The spec changes and must be re-applied. Some requirements are quantitative; others are process-based and qualitative.

Key design assumptions tested here: Evidence arrives via supplier push (not pull). The specialist reviews every supplier. Standards are imported from external sources. Compliance is assessed periodically. Suppliers are passive responders.

External grounding used: NIST OSCAL 3-layer model; W3C Verifiable Credentials 2.0 (2025); SOC 2 continuous monitoring (30%+ audit cost drop); EcoVadis fixed-schema model (3M suppliers, human-analyst-mediated); SBOM/EO 14028 lessons (ingestion gap); CSDDD Omnibus I (July 2029 deadline, fines up to 3% net turnover); Aviation AD pull model; near-miss reporting analogy; LEED tiered performance model; 60% of compliance failures from third parties (Gartner 2023); 15–30 simultaneous portals per large supplier.

1

Exception-only specialist review

Review & reporting High confidence Medium complexity

The current model assumes the specialist reviews every supplier for every requirement on every cycle. The flip: for any requirement where the DSL is fully typed, evidence is machine-readable, and AI confidence exceeds a defined threshold, auto-approve without specialist review. The specialist's caseload becomes the tail — novel evidence types, edge cases near thresholds, disputed assertions, and new requirement types where no approval precedent exists. Every other case clears automatically.

Basis & rationale

reasoned The structural problem is explicit in the grounding: "one evaluator assessing many suppliers against a shared spec." The constraint flip inverts the default. The DSL types every requirement — which means AI confidence in extracting and matching evidence values is computable, not estimated. High-confidence matches need no human in the loop. This mirrors FDA 510(k) review: cleared devices with established predicates get expedited review; novel devices get full review. The review regime matches the actual risk.

If 70–80% of requirement-evidence pairs clear automatically, a single specialist manages a supplier base that would otherwise require three or four specialists. The economics of extending compliance programs to Tier-2 and Tier-3 suppliers — currently unviable — change materially.

Downsides

Auto-approval requires high AI confidence in evidence extraction — which requires high-quality incoming evidence. Poorly structured documents from smaller suppliers will still land in the exception queue. The confidence threshold needs to be set conservatively at first and calibrated over time. There's also a regulatory risk question: for standards with external certification requirements, auto-approval may not satisfy the standard's own audit expectations. Those standards need explicit carve-outs.

Meeting test
Discussion question "What percentage of our current requirement-evidence pairs would qualify for automated approval at a high confidence threshold, and what would the specialist's actual caseload look like if that were the model?"
2

Standard authoring workbench

Standard encoding High confidence High complexity

Two friction points merge here. First: encoding a new external standard into the DSL is currently a developer task — EcoVadis's equivalent is "requires vendor development." Second: CPG buyers have proprietary requirements (animal welfare codes, antibiotic-use policies, traceability mandates) that no external standard covers, so they manage them in spreadsheets outside Athian. The solution is one workbench: AI assists in translating external standard documents into DSL drafts (paste in a PDF, get back a typed requirement set for specialist review); specialists can also compose proprietary standards from DSL building blocks directly. Both paths use the same tool.

Basis & rationale

external EcoVadis: "adding a new standard requires vendor development" — this is the named friction. NIST OSCAL's profile layer separates the requirement catalog from how it's applied to a specific program — the same separation that would let specialists compose standards from building blocks rather than authoring raw DSL. The AI compilation half is the capability that breaks the vendor-development bottleneck; the specialist-authoring half is the assumption break (standards come from the specialist, not only from external bodies).

The speed at which Athian responds to CSDDD rollout, EU DPP changes, or a customer's proprietary protocol is currently gated on developer time. A workbench changes that from weeks to hours and makes Athian's standard coverage inherently more adaptive than any competitor with a fixed schema.

Downsides

AI-assisted standard compilation will produce drafts that need careful specialist review — requirements have legal and regulatory weight, and a mis-typed threshold could create false compliance signals with real downstream consequences. The workbench needs a strong review workflow before any AI-compiled standard is published into an active compliance program. Proprietary standard authoring also increases the surface area of what Athian "owns" — if a buyer's code of conduct has errors, Athian may share liability.

Meeting test
Discussion question "How long does it currently take to encode a new standard into the DSL, and what would a standard authoring workbench mean for our ability to respond to CSDDD rollout or a customer's proprietary protocol?"
3

Supplier self-assessment against the DSL

Supplier experience High confidence Medium complexity

The current design treats suppliers as passive: they receive requirements and submit evidence in response to a cycle. The break: suppliers see their own compliance status live against the DSL and can run a self-assessment before the formal review cycle begins. They answer each typed requirement ("here is my current value / here is my supporting document"), and AI validates the submission against the DSL immediately — flagging gaps, missing evidence, or values near threshold before anything reaches the specialist queue. The specialist reviews validated submissions only, not raw assertions.

Basis & rationale

reasoned Assumption #5 in the grounding is explicit: "suppliers are passive actors responding to requirements." The break inverts the relationship. The DSL's typed requirements make self-assessment tractable — a supplier doesn't need to interpret a PDF standard; they answer structured questions with defined units. The AI validation step (before specialist review) converts the specialist's first-pass work — requesting missing data, asking for resubmission — into automated pre-screening. Rework disappears from the specialist queue.

This also changes the product relationship with suppliers from audit to coaching: a supplier who can see their compliance gaps 90 days before the review cycle has time to close them. That's a meaningfully different value proposition than "compliance checking," and it's one that reduces supplier churn from the program.

Downsides

A supplier-facing self-assessment interface is a meaningful new product surface — it needs its own UX, authentication, and notification model. There's also a risk that suppliers game the self-assessment by submitting documentation that passes AI validation but wouldn't pass specialist review. The validation logic needs to be calibrated carefully, and the specialist review of edge cases needs to remain robust. Some suppliers may resist the model if they perceive it as added burden rather than added value.

Meeting test
Discussion question "What would a supplier-facing self-assessment interface look like against the DSL, and does it change the quality of what reaches the specialist's queue?"
4

Continuous compliance state

Review & reporting Medium confidence High complexity

The annual or semi-annual review cycle is an artifact of manual assessment capacity, not a product requirement. DSL-typed requirements with thresholds and validity windows are already computable as continuous state: evidence either satisfies the requirement or it doesn't, and certificates have known expiry dates. When the DSL defines requirements this precisely, the compliance status of any supplier can be maintained in real time — gaps surface as they occur, not at the next review. Certificate expiry with 30/60/90-day lead time becomes a routine operations notification, not an audit finding.

Basis & rationale

external SOC 2 continuous monitoring: "point-in-time audit → continuous compliance state; audit costs dropped 30%+." Nuclear reactor tech specs: "operating limits create automatic shutdown triggers — continuous threshold monitoring, not periodic review." The design assumption in the grounding states explicitly: "compliance is assessed periodically (renewal cycle)." This is the assumption being tested. The DSL already encodes units, thresholds, and time windows — the data model for continuous monitoring is already present; the question is whether the platform surfaces it as live state or batched review.

The risk exposure window between annual audits — where a supplier could be out of compliance for months before anyone knows — disappears with continuous state. This is the same structural shift SOC 2 made, applied to supply chain.

Downsides

Continuous compliance state requires continuous evidence — which today means suppliers push evidence periodically, or the platform pulls from third-party sources. Without a reliable evidence stream, "continuous state" is just a live view of stale data. This is architecturally dependent on idea 7 (pull model for evidence) being partly solved. The medium confidence rating reflects this dependency. There's also a notification fatigue risk: continuous monitoring produces alerts, and a compliance specialist whose dashboard is always active needs a good alert prioritization model to avoid burn-out.

Meeting test
Discussion question "Is the review cycle a deliberate product choice or an inherited assumption from manual processes, and what would continuous compliance state mean for how we build the review queue?"
5

Compliance output as CSDDD regulatory filing

Review & reporting Medium confidence Medium complexity

Buyers use Athian to manage supplier compliance. The same structured, DSL-typed compliance records are also the input their Scope 3 reporting requires — for CSDDD, EU DPP, and similar regulatory frameworks with mandatory machine-readable supply chain data. If Athian's compliance output is structured to satisfy these reporting requirements directly (an export in the required schema, a signed assertion per requirement type), buyers move from manual compilation to data export. Athian stops being a compliance tool and becomes regulatory reporting infrastructure — a significantly harder category to replace.

Basis & rationale

external CSDDD Omnibus I: scope narrowed but deadline confirmed July 2029 (financial years beginning Jan 2028 for largest companies), fines up to 3% of net turnover. EU DPP: "mandatory machine-readable supply chain data." W3C Verifiable Credentials 2.0 (2025): "cryptographically verifiable compliance assertions." The compliance data Athian already generates — typed, evidence-backed, time-windowed — is structurally close to what both frameworks require. The gap is in output format and schema conformance, not in the underlying data model.

The leverage: if Athian is the system of record for a buyer's Scope 3 supplier compliance data, and Athian can generate the buyer's CSDDD filing directly, switching cost becomes structural. No compliance tool competes on features when you are the filing system.

Downsides

CSDDD compliance requirements are still in flux — Omnibus I narrowed scope and the exact machine-readable data requirements aren't fully published. Building output formats for frameworks that are still being finalized risks building to an interim specification. The medium confidence rating reflects this regulatory uncertainty. There's also a legal exposure question: if Athian generates a CSDDD filing and it contains errors that result in fines, who bears responsibility? Legal review of the product positioning would be needed before framing this as "regulatory filing infrastructure."

Meeting test
Discussion question "Does the output structure of our compliance assessments map to what buyers need for CSDDD and EU DPP reporting, and is positioning Athian as regulatory filing infrastructure a product direction worth pursuing in the 2026–2028 window?"
6

Near-miss / compliance drift scoring

Review & reporting High confidence Low complexity

The compliance platform surfaces pass/fail states. It doesn't currently surface how close a supplier is to failing — or whether they're trending toward failure. A supplier who hit 15.2% methane reduction when 15% is required is a different risk profile than one who hit 22%. A DSL-typed requirement with a numeric threshold has a continuous distance-to-failure metric already computable from evidence values. Tracking that metric over cycles produces a drift signal: suppliers trending toward threshold (not just below it) get flagged for proactive engagement. The specialist's review queue becomes risk-ranked rather than schedule-ranked.

Basis & rationale

reasoned Aviation near-miss reporting: near-miss data is a leading indicator of system failure; the value is proactive, not reactive. "60% of compliance failures originate from third parties" (Gartner 2023) — but the current compliance system only surfaces failures after they occur. For quantitative DSL requirements, distance-to-failure is already computable from the extracted evidence value and the threshold. Tracking it over time is a data modeling and display problem, not an AI problem. The low complexity rating reflects this — the data is there; the question is whether it's surfaced.

Downsides

Drift scoring requires multiple cycles of evidence to be meaningful — it's a lagging signal in the first year. Suppliers who have only completed one review cycle have no trend to analyze. There's also a risk that near-miss scoring creates anxiety for suppliers who are technically compliant — if a supplier at 16% (vs 15% threshold) sees themselves flagged as high risk, the relationship implications need to be managed carefully. The scoring should be internal to the specialist's view, not visible to suppliers.

Meeting test
Discussion question "Do we store the distance between extracted evidence values and DSL thresholds across cycles, and could that become a proactive risk-ranking signal for the specialist's review queue?"
7

Portable compliance passport (Verifiable Credentials)

Supplier experience Medium confidence High complexity

Every supplier who completes a compliance cycle against a DSL-encoded standard produces a structured, typed compliance record: assertions with values, units, evidence, and a specialist approval. That record, issued as a W3C Verifiable Credential, is portable and cryptographically verifiable by any other buyer without re-verification. A supplier compliant with one CPG buyer's Athian program could present that credential to a second CPG buyer running a compatible standard — without resubmitting evidence or re-entering data. Portal fatigue (15–30 portals per large supplier) is reduced because the credential travels with the supplier rather than being re-established per buyer.

Basis & rationale

external W3C Verifiable Credentials 2.0 published 2025; "GS1 working group on end-to-end traceability with VCs and EPCIS; UN Transparency Protocol adopted VCs for greenwashing prevention." EcoVadis has 3M suppliers in its network. The structural advantage of a portable credential is what could let Athian scale past EcoVadis's human-analyst-mediated model without matching their supplier count first. The network effect compounds: each new buyer who adopts Athian increases the value of an Athian credential to suppliers already in the network — a supplier who has already done the work once doesn't want to do it again for buyer #2, and a portable credential removes that friction.

Downsides

Credential portability requires standardization at the DSL level across buyers — two buyers with slightly different requirement definitions for the same standard produce credentials that aren't interoperable. This is architecturally tractable but organizationally difficult (it requires buyer coordination). The VC infrastructure itself (issuance, revocation, wallet model for suppliers) is high complexity and has limited production-ready tooling today outside of government-adjacent use cases. This is a 2027+ play, not a near-term feature. The medium confidence rating reflects real execution uncertainty despite strong strategic logic.

Meeting test
Discussion question "Does our DSL-generated compliance record map cleanly to a VC structure, and what would it mean for supplier adoption if a single Athian assessment was reusable across multiple buyers?"
